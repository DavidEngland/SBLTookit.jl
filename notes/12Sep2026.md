### **Physical & Thermodynamic Feedback Mechanics**

1. **The "Zero-Curtain" Freezing Plateau (\(C_s(T_s)\)):**
   When surface skin temperature drops through the freezing threshold (\(T_m \approx 273.15\text{ K}\) / \(0^\circ\text{C}\)), soil moisture undergoes phase-change freezing. This releases latent heat of fusion (\(L_f\)), producing a temporary spike in the apparent soil heat capacity \(C_s(T_s) = C_{\text{base}} + \rho_w w_0 L_f \cdot \delta_{\sigma}(T_s - T_m)\). As shown in Panel A and Panel C, this thermal buffer creates a distinct **zero-curtain plateau** that arrests surface temperature drops near \(0^\circ\text{C}\) for several hours until the liquid water fraction freezes.

2. **Frost Deposition Latent Heat Warming (\(LE_0 < 0\)):**
   Under humid polar conditions, downward vapor transport drives frost deposition (\(LE_0 = \rho L_s K_q \frac{q_1 - q_s(T_s)}{z_1} < 0\)). Because frost deposition transports moisture downward, it releases latent heat of sublimation directly onto the surface skin, acting as a secondary thermodynamic warming source that offsets net longwave radiative loss (\(R_n = -45\text{ W/m}^2\)).

3. **C-Z0HR Turbulence Coupling & Feedback Attenuation:**
   In unregularized closures (\(\mathcal{G} > 1\)), steep stability function derivatives collapse eddy diffusivities (\(K_h, K_q \to 0\)), shutting off both sensible heat flux (\(H_0 \to 0\)) and latent heat flux (\(LE_0 \to 0\)). Under C-Z0HR regularization (\(\mathcal{G}_{\text{reg}} < 1\)), continuous turbulent exchange (\(K_h, K_q > 0\)) maintains dual atmospheric heat delivery (\(H_0 + LE_0\)), which works in tandem with soil moisture freezing to prevent unphysical runaway land-surface cold biases.

### Key Mathematical Diagnostics Visualized

* **Fixed-Point Preservation [Eq. (5)]:** At $x = 0$ ($Ri_g = Ri_c$), the mapping collapses to $\widetilde{Ri}_g = Ri_c + 0 = Ri_c$, guaranteeing that critical stability thresholds remain physically exact regardless of curvature magnitude $C_\eta$.
* **Analytical Slope Bound [Theorem 1]:** Differentiating $\widetilde{Ri}_g$ with respect to $Ri_g$ under fixed local curvature yields:

$$\mathcal{D}_\alpha^{(R)}(x) = \frac{\Phi_\epsilon(x) + C_\eta}{\Phi_\epsilon(x) + (1+\alpha)C_\eta} + \frac{\alpha C_\eta x^2}{\Phi_\epsilon(x) \left[ \Phi_\epsilon(x) + (1+\alpha)C_\eta \right]^2}$$

At the core inversion center ($x \to 0$), the second term vanishes, yielding $\mathcal{D}_\alpha^{(R)}(0) = \frac{\epsilon + C_\eta}{\epsilon + (1+\alpha)C_\eta} \approx \frac{1}{1+\alpha} = \frac{1}{3}$.

* **Asymptotic Recovery:** As $\vert{}x\vert{} \gg C_\eta$, $\Phi_\epsilon(x) \approx \vert{}x\vert{} \gg C_\eta$, causing $\mathcal{D}_\alpha^{(R)}(x) \to 1.0$, restoring standard unregularized model behavior outside high-curvature shear layers.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from matplotlib.colors import TwoSlopeNorm

# Set random seed for reproducibility of subtle turbulent noise
np.random.seed(42)

# Grid definition
t = np.linspace(0, 24, 240)  # Hours
z = np.linspace(0, 200, 200) # Height (m)
T_grid, Z_grid = np.meshgrid(t, z)

# Nocturnal phase envelope (strongest between t=6h and t=18h)
night_mask = np.sin(np.pi * np.clip(T_grid - 4, 0, 16) / 16) ** 1.5

# -------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Synthetic Physics - Panel 1: Unregularized Baseline
# -------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Runaway surface cold pool (bottom 40m)
cold_pool_unreg = -4.5 * np.exp(-Z_grid / 22.0) * night_mask

# Abrupt turbulence shutoff kink at z ~ 50-70m
kink_z = 55.0 + 12.0 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * T_grid / 6.0)
shutoff_unreg = 0.5 * (1.0 + np.tanh((Z_grid - kink_z) / 4.0))

# Discontinuous step-change temperature field
temp_unreg = cold_pool_unreg * (1.0 - 0.75 * shutoff_unreg)
# Add numerical ringing/step discontinuities near inversion cap
ringing = 0.35 * np.exp(-((Z_grid - 65) / 10)**2) * np.sin(2 * np.pi * T_grid / 1.5) * (night_mask > 0.2)
temp_unreg += ringing

# Eddy diffusivity K_m for Unregularized (abrupt drop to 0)
Km_unreg = 2.5 * (Z_grid / 50.0) * np.exp(-Z_grid / 40.0) * (1.0 - night_mask * 0.95)
Km_unreg[Z_grid > kink_z] *= 0.02  # Spurious sudden quenching above kink

# -------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Synthetic Physics - Panel 2: C-Z0HR Regularized
# -------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Mitigated cold bias (-1.1 K at surface), deep continuous heat flux
cold_pool_cz0hr = -1.1 * np.exp(-Z_grid / 65.0) * night_mask

# Smooth vertical diffusion profile without sharp step-changes
temp_cz0hr = cold_pool_cz0hr

# Eddy diffusivity K_m for C-Z0HR (smooth continuous residual tail)
Km_cz0hr = 2.5 * (Z_grid / 50.0) * np.exp(-Z_grid / 60.0) * (1.0 - night_mask * 0.65)
Km_cz0hr = np.maximum(Km_cz0hr, 0.08)  # C-Z0HR non-zero regularized floor

# -------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Plotting
# -------------------------------------------------------------------------
fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 5.0), dpi=300, sharey=True)

# Color scale setup
vmin, vmax = -4.8, 0.5
cmap = plt.cm.RdBu_r
norm = TwoSlopeNorm(vmin=vmin, vcenter=-1.5, vmax=vmax)

# --- Panel (a): Unregularized ---
cf1 = ax1.contourf(T_grid, Z_grid, temp_unreg, levels=35, cmap=cmap, norm=norm, extend='both')
# Contour lines for Km shutoff threshold
cs1 = ax1.contour(T_grid, Z_grid, Km_unreg, levels=[0.05], colors='black', linestyles='--', linewidths=1.2)
ax1.clabel(cs1, inline=True, fmt=r'$K_m \approx 0$', fontsize=8)

### 2. Step-by-Step Chain Decomposition

The discrete feedback loop gain \(\mathcal{G}\) across a 1D column model is defined as the product of five sequential sensitivity multipliers:

\[\mathcal{G} = \underbrace{\left(\frac{\partial Ri_g}{\partial \theta_z}\right)}_{\text{Diagnostic Gain}} \cdot \underbrace{\left(\frac{\partial \theta_z}{\partial H}\right)}_{\text{Process Impedance}} \cdot \underbrace{\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial K_h}\right)}_{\text{Flux Actuator}} \cdot \underbrace{\mathcal{K}_h'(Ri_g^{\text{reg}})}_{\text{Closure Slope}} \cdot \underbrace{\mathcal{D}_\alpha^{(R)}(Ri_g)}_{\text{Regularizer Damping}}\]

#### **1. Diagnostic Sensor Gain (\(\partial Ri_g / \partial \theta_z\))**

* **Engineering:** Sensor sensitivity. Near weak shear, this acts as a high-gain amplifier.
* **Atmospheric/NWP:** Evaluates to \(\frac{g}{\theta_0 S^2}\). Near the nose of a Low-Level Jet where wind shear vanishes (\(S \to 0\)), this term scales as \(\mathcal{O}(S^{-2})\). Unregularized gradient calculations cause noise power to blow up as \(\mathcal{O}(S^{-6})\), injecting massive diagnostic variance into the feedback loop.

#### **2. Process Transfer Impedance (\(\partial \theta_z / \partial H\))**

* **Engineering:** Plant transfer impedance relating heat flux input to lapse rate output.
* **Atmospheric/NWP:** Relates vertical sensible heat flux divergence (\(\frac{\partial H}{\partial z}\)) to the evolution of the near-surface potential temperature gradient \(\theta_z \approx \frac{\theta_1 - T_s}{z_1}\) across vertical grid spacing \(z_1\).

#### **3. Flux Actuator Gain (\(\partial H / \partial K_h\))**

* **Engineering:** Direct actuator sensitivity relating control variable \(K_h\) to output flux \(H\).
* **Atmospheric/NWP:** Evaluates to \(-\rho c_p \theta_z\). Dictates how strongly downward sensible heat flux \(H_0 = -\rho c_p K_h \theta_z\) responds to changes in turbulent eddy diffusivity \(K_h\).

#### **4. Closure Slope (\(\mathcal{K}_h'(Ri_g)\))**

* **Engineering:** Non-linear actuator transfer characteristic.
* **Atmospheric/NWP:** The derivative of the empirical stability function (e.g., Businger–Dyer or 1.5-order TKE closures). Near critical Richardson numbers (\(Ri_c \approx 0.20\)), steep short-tail closures exhibit \(\mathcal{K}_h' \to -\infty\), acting as an aggressive step-down switch that cuts off mixing.

#### **5. Regularizer Damping Factor (\(\mathcal{D}_\alpha^{(R)}\))**

* **Engineering:** Low-pass gain attenuator inserted into the feedback path.
* **Atmospheric/NWP:** Evaluates the partial slope of the regularized mapping \(\left.\frac{\partial Ri_g^{\text{reg}}}{\partial Ri_g}\right|_C\). Under spatial profile curvature \(C(z, \Delta z) = \frac{1}{2}|\partial^2 Ri_g / \partial z^2|(\Delta z)^2 + \epsilon_c Ri_c\), the slope collapses at \(Ri_g = Ri_c\) to:
  \[\mathcal{D}_\alpha^{(R)}(Ri_c) \approx \frac{1}{1 + \alpha} \tag{6}\]
  For standard damping control \(\alpha = 2.0\), this attenuates sensitivity by \(66.7\%\) (\(\mathcal{D}_2^{(R)} = 1/3\)), capping the overall gain chain.

Here's the core idea, stripped of the control-theory framing:

**The problem:** In weather models, there's a feedback loop near the ground at night (or in calm, stable conditions) that can spiral out of control numerically.

Here's the chain of events: when the air near the ground is very stable (warm air sitting on top of cold air, with little wind mixing things up), the model's turbulence scheme decides there's essentially no mixing happening — it sets the "eddy diffusivity" (basically, how much heat gets stirred between the ground and the air above) to nearly zero. That cuts off the flow of heat from the atmosphere down to the ground. With no heat coming in, the ground keeps radiating heat away and cooling. But a colder ground makes the air-to-ground temperature difference even sharper — which makes the model even _more_ convinced there's no mixing, so it clamps the heat flow down further. Round and round it goes.

This is a classic positive feedback loop, the same kind of runaway instability engineers worry about in control systems (think microphone feedback screech, but for a heat budget). Left unchecked, it produces a surface temperature that's way too cold compared to reality — the paper cites errors of 3-4°C, which is a big deal for forecast accuracy.

**The fix described:** Add a "damping" term that softens how abruptly the turbulence scheme shuts off mixing right at the critical threshold. Instead of slamming the heat flow to zero, it tapers it down more gently. Mathematically, this caps how much the feedback loop can amplify itself on each pass, keeping it below the runaway threshold — while still letting the model correctly predict _no_ turbulence far away from that critical point (i.e., it doesn't break the physics, it just smooths the numerical cliff edge). The claimed result is shrinking that cold-bias error from -3.2°C down to about -0.1°C in a standard test case (GABLS3).

**The engineering analogy** is basically borrowed vocabulary: this is being described as "loop gain," "sensor amplification," and "actuator slope" the same way you'd analyze whether a feedback control circuit is stable, just relabeled with atmospheric variables (Richardson number, heat flux, potential temperature gradient) standing in for the electrical/mechanical ones.

### **1. Shared Physical Foundation: Eliminating Spurious Quenching**

Both frameworks address the same fundamental pathology in geophysical fluid dynamics (GFD): **spurious thermal quenching and runaway surface cold biases**.

* **The Problem:** Classical Monin–Obukhov Similarity Theory (MOST) stability functions exhibit steep short-tailed cutoffs near the critical Richardson number (\(Ri_c \approx 0.20\text{--}0.25\)). In discretized atmospheric models, coarse vertical grid spacing (\(\Delta z\)) fails to resolve sharp near-surface gradients or jet-nose curvature, causing discretized \(Ri_g\) to artificially cross \(Ri_c\).
* **The Physical Consequence:** This artificial threshold crossing shuts off turbulent diffusivities (\(K_h \to 0\)), decoupling the ground skin temperature (\(T_s\)) from the warm boundary layer aloft. In polar and nocturnal regimes, this suppresses entrainment warming—where heat from aloft is mixed downward—and forces models into unphysical surface cold-bias divergence.
* **The Shared Philosophy:** Neither framework advocates replacing short-tail MOST functions with ad-hoc "long-tail" empirical curves (e.g., Louis or Beljaars–Holtslag) on fine grids. Both insist that short-tail functions represent true local similarity physics, requiring instead that **grid spacing (\(\Delta z\)) and profile curvature (\(\partial^2 Ri_g / \partial z^2\)) be explicitly integrated into the mathematical operator**.

### **2. Core Conceptual Convergences**

#### **A. Centrality of Vertical Profile Curvature (\(\partial^2 Ri_g / \partial z^2\))**

* **McNider & Pour-Biazar (2026):** In solving their grid-correction ordinary differential equation (\(\frac{d}{d\Delta z}[f_s f_c] = 0\)), they prove that if vertical potential temperature and velocity gradients are strictly linear (\(\frac{\partial^2 Ri_g}{\partial z^2} = 0\)), discretization across \(\Delta z\) is exact and no correction is needed. Consequently, they make their grid-adjustment weight \(D\) explicitly dependent on profile curvature: \(D = D_0 + M \left| \frac{\partial^2 Ri_g}{\partial z^2} \right|\).
* **C-Z0HR / GSPT:** C-Z0HR directly embeds grid-resolved spatial curvature \(C(z, \Delta z) = \frac{1}{2} \left| \frac{\partial^2 Ri_g}{\partial z^2} \right| (\Delta z)^2 + \epsilon_c Ri_c\) into the upstream Richardson mapping \(Ri_g^{\text{reg}}\). Both frameworks independently arrive at the conclusion that **grid dependence in \(Ri\)-space is fundamentally driven by profile curvature interacting with grid cell thickness**.

#### **B. Preserving Baseline MOST Parameters Without Retuning**

Both methodologies eliminate the practice of retuning Monin–Obukhov empirical constants (\(\beta = 5.0\)) or critical Richardson numbers to match distorted coarse-grid soundings. By absorbing grid and geometric distortions into the mathematical operator, legacy closures retain their exact, validated parameter values.
